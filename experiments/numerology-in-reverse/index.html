Numerology in Reverse: Can Three Numbers Reveal Your Name?

An experiment with U.S. and Japanese names, looking for the clues left in the numbers

In numerology, you can turn a name into numbers and use them for a reading.

But can you go back from those numbers to the name?

Take 9 · 22 · 5.

In the U.S. name data for births in 2024 that I examined, 13 distinct names produced these three numbers. Yet a single name, Theodore, accounted for about 98.3% of the people listed under those matching names.

I haven't asked for a name. Just the calculation results.

“Theodore, by any chance?”

For someone drawn from the population represented by the data, some combinations make that a remarkably strong guess.

Meanwhile, the Japanese names Mayu and Ayumi both give 6 · 4 · 11. Those three numbers cannot tell the two apart. An initial, M or A, can.

The calculation is the same. What changes is how the names gather around its results.

For this experiment, I set aside numerology's interpretations and asked: given the calculation rules and the results, how far can we narrow down the original name? I also built a tool for exploring the candidates.

We'll start with U.S. names and the difference between counting names and counting people. Then we'll look at Japanese names, initials, and the difficulty of building a useful name list. Finally, we'll find out why the three numbers cannot be chosen freely in the first place.

You can jump to whichever part interests you:

1. Turning a name into numbers

Numerology has various conventions. Here, I'll leave its history and interpretations aside and use only the calculation that adds up the letters of a name.

The target is a given name, not a full name including a surname. For the U.S. data, I use the alphabetic spellings in the published records. For Japanese names, I convert their readings into Roman letters.

The letter values follow what is called the Pythagorean system.

Value Letters
1 A · J · S
2 B · K · T
3 C · L · U
4 D · M · V
5 E · N · W
6 F · O · X
7 G · P · Y
8 H · Q · Z
9 I · R

It's simply 1 through 9, repeated along the alphabet.

From one name, we make three sums:

Symbol Letters included Name of the number
E All letters Expression
S Vowels only Soul Urge
P Consonants only Personality

In this experiment, A, E, I, O, and U are vowels. Y is a consonant.

When a sum is large, we make it smaller by adding its digits. For example, 15 becomes 1 + 5 = 6.

For the examples in the main article, however, we stop if we reach 11, 22, or 33. These are called master numbers; some numerology interpretations associate them with exceptional potential and the challenges that accompany it. Here, what matters is the rule that the calculation stops at those values.

We add all the letter values first, then start adding digits. We don't reduce the running total after each letter.

For THEODORE, the name from the opening example, the calculation is:

Letters included Addition Sum → result
All letters: THEODORE 2 + 8 + 5 + 6 + 4 + 6 + 9 + 5 45 → 9
Vowels: EOOE 5 + 6 + 6 + 5 22 → 22
Consonants: THDR 2 + 8 + 4 + 9 23 → 5

That gives 9 · 22 · 5, in E, S, P order.

2. To work backwards, calculate everything first

A name may determine its numbers, but several names might lead to the same result. So before trying to pick one answer, I decided to find all the candidates.

Collect real names, calculate E, S, and P for each, and put names with the same results into the same box.

When someone enters “9 · 22 · 5,” open that box. If we also know their initial, keep only the names that begin with that letter.

That's the whole mechanism. It looks for matches in its name list rather than generating unknown names.

And that gives us our first important limitation:

A name that isn't on the list will never appear, however correctly we calculate its numbers.

Working backwards and knowing the name in the first place are separate problems.

3. U.S. names: 13 candidates doesn't mean a one-in-13 chance

Let's begin with the U.S. data, where we have counts for each name.

I used a public copy of national name data for births in 2024, originating from the Social Security Administration (SSA). Combining the male and female counts for each spelling gives 29,225 distinct spellings and a total listed count of 3,328,501.

The names are included regardless of their linguistic origin: these are alphabetic given-name spellings appearing in the U.S. records.

Of these, 13 names produce 9 · 22 · 5. If each distinct name gets equal weight, each candidate has a one-in-13 chance, or about 7.7%.

Count the people listed under those names, though, and the picture changes.

Name Listed count matching this combination
Theodore 12,024
The other 12 names combined 205
Total 12,229

Theodore's share is:

\[ \frac{12,024}{12,229}\approx 98.3\% \]

There are 13 candidate names, but about 98% of the matching people in the data share just one of them.

Theodore was the fourth most popular boy's name nationwide that year. A name that was already common became overwhelmingly dominant among names with this particular numerical result.

Are we choosing a name, or choosing a person? That alone makes a big difference to how likely a guess is to be right.

But are all the boxes this helpful? Let's try the whole dataset.

For each box defined by the available clues, we answer with the name that has the highest listed count. Add those winning counts across all boxes, then divide by the total listed count: that gives the “top candidate” rate in the table below. For the top 10, we select up to 10 names with the largest counts in each box and tally them in the same way.

In the final row, we assume that we also know the sex recorded in the source data, as well as the initial.

Information available Top candidate is correct Correct name is in the top 10
No additional clues 0.67% 4.45%
Initial only 7.29% 33.94%
Three numbers only 22.74% 66.19%
Three numbers + initial 64.33% 96.10%
Three numbers + initial + recorded sex 72.48% 98.02%

Using the rule that preserves 11, 22, and 33. Sex is unspecified in the first four rows. Candidates are ranked using counts from this same dataset, and those counts also supply the weights for the evaluation.

Even without the numbers or an initial, we can guess names in order of popularity. Compared with that starting point, the numbers and initial are clearly useful.

Adding recorded sex raises the top-candidate success rate by about 8 percentage points. A spelling listed under both male and female records remains a candidate under either condition, ranked using the count for the selected category.

The 96.10% and 98.02% figures describe how often the correct name falls within the top 10 when we select a person from this dataset and use the corresponding clues. They don't tell us how well this would work for another generation or for names missing from the data.

So far, the numbers have gathered the candidates, and the counts have decided which names to guess first. How far can we get with a list that has no counts? Let's turn to Japanese names.

4. Japanese names: MAYU and AYUMI give the same numbers

For Japanese names, the calculation uses a reading written in Roman letters, rather than the shapes or stroke counts of the kanji characters.

The program uses a convention based on Hepburn romanization, retaining the vowel letters used to represent long vowels. For example, ゆうき becomes YUUKI, ゆき becomes YUKI, and しおり becomes SHIORI. All Japanese results below use this convention.

Mayu (まゆ) becomes MAYU, and Ayumi (あゆみ) becomes AYUMI. Let's add a name whose spelling looks rather different: Nodoka (のどか), or NODOKA.

Name All-letter sum → E Vowel sum → S Consonant sum → P
MAYU 15 → 6 4 → 4 11 → 11
AYUMI 24 → 6 13 → 4 11 → 11
NODOKA 24 → 6 13 → 4 11 → 11

All three give 6 · 4 · 11.

Different lengths. Different sequences of letters. Yet all three results match.

AYUMI has the same M, A, Y, and U as MAYU, plus I, whose value is 9. Both the total and vowel sums increase by 9, but in this example, adding the digits takes us back to the same results. And since we're adding, the order of the letters is lost too.

This isn't limited to similar spellings. The only letter MAYU and NODOKA share is A. Their consonants, M and Y versus N, D, and K, are entirely different, but both consonant sums are 11.

Some information has slipped away. A name determines its numbers; the numbers don't necessarily determine a single name.

Add “the initial is M,” and AYUMI and NODOKA disappear. With A, MAYU and NODOKA disappear. For these three names, one letter tells them apart.

What happens when we're dealing with tens of thousands of names? How many candidates will the numbers and an initial leave us with?

5. Japanese names: how far can we narrow down more than 30,000 readings?

For the original Japanese candidate list, I selected given-name entries from the proper-name dictionary JMnedict.

After excluding surnames and full-name entries, processing the written readings under a fixed set of rules, and retaining those the program could calculate, the study contained 33,837 distinct readings.

A type here means one reading: the pronunciation represented in kana. Different kanji spellings of the same reading do not create additional candidates.

These sources do not provide a consistent set of counts for how many people throughout Japan have each reading. So I gave all 33,837 readings equal probability, and for each name asked: how many candidates share its clues?

Information available Mean candidate count Median candidate count
Nothing 33,837 33,837
Three numbers only 303.2 303
Initial only 2,737.9 2,333
Three numbers + initial 26.6 22

Using the rule that preserves 11, 22, and 33. These results use the full original dictionary, before selecting candidates for priority display. Names without vowels or without consonants are included.

The three numbers alone reduce the average from more than 30,000 candidates to about 303. Add one initial, and it falls to about 27.

That's a list I'd like to see.

The numbers and initial identify a unique candidate for only about 1.4% of the readings. Most still have company.

In this average, a very common name and a name you rarely encounter each get one vote. The weighting differs from choosing a person on the street, so the Japanese version does not display a population-based probability of a candidate being correct.

The experiment with sex as an additional clue was limited to the U.S. data, where counts by recorded sex were available. For the Japanese candidate dictionary, I have not mapped and validated sex information for each reading, and do not have a corresponding set of counts by sex.

6. Japanese names: the list works, but some names look unfamiliar

Looking through the actual candidates revealed another problem. Being in a dictionary is not the same as being a name you encounter every day.

There were unfamiliar readings, old-fashioned readings, and readings that looked like names from other languages. Being unusual doesn't make a name wrong. Still, for a list presented as “Is your name in here?”, I wanted something easier to take in.

So I kept the original dictionary and selected which candidates to show first.

Readings that also appeared in a separate public name list received priority. This produced a priority set of 8,244 readings.

It is not a list of the “top so-many percent of Japanese names.” It is a display selection based on appearances in the reference sources. Nor are the priority candidates ordered by their share of the population.

For 6 · 4 · 11, the combination shared by MAYU and AYUMI, the results were:

Clues Matches in the original dictionary Priority candidates
6 · 4 · 11 192 53
6 · 4 · 11 + M 26 5
6 · 4 · 11 + A 14 3

With M, the priority candidates are:

Masatada, Matsumi, Mayu, Muga, and Monako.

With A:

Ayataka, Akuri, and Ayumi.

MAYU and AYUMI have indeed parted company. Neither list, though, gives us just one name. And because selection is based on appearances in reference sources, it doesn't leave only familiar names.

Here's another example. For 9 · 7 · 11 with the initial A, the original dictionary contains 20 candidates. Five receive priority:

Ayumu, Atori, Ayame, Ariko, and Arito.

With this article's romanization and fixed list, preserving 11, 22, and 33 and specifying an initial brought the first set of candidates shown to a maximum of 25.

But in that final step from 26 candidates to five, the numbers had supplied no extra information. Part of the reduction came from deciding which names to show.

A smaller list does not guarantee that the right name is still in it. That's why the tool also lets you look at the other candidates or the full candidate list.

7. Japanese names: I can't find my own name

Now that plausible lists were appearing, I tried a few more searches.

My own name was nowhere among the candidates I was looking at.

A little awkward when you're the one making the tool.

I searched the original list in Excel. My reading was there. It was one of the 33,837 candidates.

What I found was that the Roman-letter spelling I normally entered differed from the spelling the dictionary used for its calculation. In my case, the two spellings also produced different numbers.

To illustrate the mechanism, let's use こうた as an example. The dictionary calculates it as KOUTA. Under the rule that preserves 11, 22, and 33, comparing it with KOTA gives:

Spelling All-letter sum → E Vowel sum → S Consonant sum → P
KOUTA 14 → 5 10 → 1 4 → 4
KOTA 11 → 11 7 → 7 4 → 4

One U changes 5 · 1 · 4 into 11 · 7 · 4.

A search for 11 · 7 · 4, calculated from KOTA, will not match the reading stored as KOUTA. The name is in the dictionary, but we're opening the wrong box.

KOTA is a perfectly ordinary spelling to use. Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs guidance for passport romanization explicitly gives コウタ → KOTA as an example. Different purposes call for different romanization conventions.

Numerology calculators also offer examples of both approaches: some omit the extra vowel letters in long vowels, while others retain them following the kana spelling.

I added a feature that compares the existing spelling with a passport-style version generated from the hiragana reading. This is an approximation based on basic passport rules, not a determination of the spelling on someone's actual passport. The reverse search can also include passport-style spellings, allowing it to find candidates under either convention.

When I checked the conditions and searched again, I found my own name too.

It had been in the dictionary. But having the same reading on the list did not necessarily lead me to the same box. The spelling used for the calculation needed checking as well.

8. Why do so many combinations return no results?

Let's return to the calculation itself, which is shared by both the U.S. and Japanese searches.

Once the tool exists, it's tempting to try arbitrary numbers.

“What about this combination?”

No results.

“And this one?”

Still no results.

It turned out that gaps in the name list were not the whole explanation.

The three numbers were never free to vary independently.

Go back to the original addition. Let V be the vowel sum, K the consonant sum, and T the total. Then:

\[ T=V+K \]

We've only split the letters into vowels and consonants, so this is obvious once you look at it.

The relationship leaves a trace even after we add the digits.

First, consider the rule that always reduces to one digit, without treating 11, 22, or 33 as special. If d reduces a positive integer n to one digit, then:

\[ d(n)=1+((n-1)\bmod 9) \]

Here, “mod” means the remainder after division.

Why 9? For example, the difference between 10a + b and its digit sum a + b is 9a. Adding digits removes a multiple of 9, so the remainder on division by 9 stays the same.

For names containing both vowels and consonants, this gives:

\[ E=d(S+P) \]

Once S and P are fixed, E is fixed too.

If all three range from 1 to 9, there seem to be 9³ = 729 combinations. But only 9² = 81 satisfy this relationship, one for each choice of S and P.

Having three numbers did not mean having three independent clues.

9. Keeping 11, 22, and 33 doesn't remove the constraint

What happens under the rule used in the main examples, where we stop at 11, 22, or 33?

S and P no longer necessarily determine a single E. But adding digits still preserves the remainder on division by 9.

So, when a name has both vowels and consonants, we must at least have:

\[ E\equiv S+P\pmod 9 \]

In words: E and S + P must have the same remainder when divided by 9.

Take S = 4 and P = 11. Their sum is 15, whose remainder on division by 9 is 6.

Among the possible values of E, namely 1–9, 11, 22, and 33, only 6 and 33 have that remainder.

Saying “it can only be 6” would be a mistake.

Suppose the unreduced vowel sum is 4 and the consonant sum is 29. Then:

\[ S=4,\qquad P:29\longrightarrow11,\qquad E:4+29=33 \]

In the Japanese dictionary used here, however, 6 · 4 · 11 had 192 candidates, while 33 · 4 · 11 had none.

A result being arithmetically possible and a name with that result appearing in the dictionary are different matters.

The combination 33 · 4 · 11 was absent from this dictionary. That alone doesn't establish that it is impossible for Japanese names in general.

For combinations in which all three components are numeric, the counts are:

What we count Combinations
Freely choosing each of three numbers from 12 values 1,728
Fail the remainder condition 1,536
Pass the remainder condition 192
Actually have candidates in this Japanese dictionary 177

The 192 combinations pass the remainder test. That is not a proof that every one of them can be realized.

Still, if we try the 1,728 inputs uniformly, only about 10.2% return a candidate. No wonder there were so many empty searches. This percentage concerns input combinations, not the share of people covered by the name list.

Adding the 10 combinations with candidates but no vowel or no consonant component gives 187 occupied combinations in the Japanese dictionary. The candidate counts, including these cases, are listed in Appendix 2 on a separate page.

11. How much of a name was left in the numbers?

Turn a name into a sum, and letter order disappears. Different letters and different name lengths can collapse into the same results. The three numbers are related to one another, and they don't always lead back to one name.

Yet they hadn't lost everything.

For the U.S. data, combining the numbers, initial, and listed counts placed the correct name in the top 10 for about 96% of the people listed in that same dataset. Knowing recorded sex raised that to about 98%. In the original Japanese dictionary, the numbers alone left an average of about 303 candidates; an initial brought that down to about 27.

The neat lists on screen also depended on the dictionary and display choices. Making those lists easier to read left some names outside the first view. For Japanese names, even the Roman-letter spelling used before calculating the numbers needed to be consistent.

At first, I wanted to guess a name from its numbers.

But as I opened the boxes, I became curious about the names gathering inside them.

The numbers collect the candidates. The first letter separates them further. If we know how many people have each name, the order of our guesses changes. Every name on the screen has a reason for still being there.

“Is your name …?”

I set out to find one name and ended up discovering many. Behind the same three numbers were all these different names.

Appendix 1: candidate sets, proportions, and information

This part is for readers who would like a closer look at the calculations.

Collecting candidates and calculating their shares

Let D be the set of names, and let f be the function that calculates a name's three numbers. The candidates for a result z form the set:

\[ C(z)=\{x\in D\mid f(x)=z\} \]

In words, collect every name in D whose calculation gives z. This is the “box” used throughout the article.

Choose a name X from the U.S. data with probability proportional to its listed count, and let Z be its calculation result. If n(x) is the listed count for name x, then its share given the numbers z is:

\[ P(X=x\mid Z=z) =\frac{n(x)}{\sum_{y\in C(z)}n(y)} \qquad(x\in C(z)) \]

If we also know the initial, recalculate the denominator using only candidates that begin with that letter. If recorded sex is specified, use the counts for that category. These calculations apply when at least one candidate matches the conditions.

Average candidate count when each name has equal weight

The mean candidate counts in the article do not come from choosing number combinations uniformly. We choose the correct name uniformly from the distinct names, then count how many candidates share that name's clues.

If the groups of names sharing a result have sizes n₁, n₂, …, and the total number of candidate names is N, then:

\[ \text{Mean candidate count}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_g n_g^2 \]

A group of size n_g appears n_g times when we go through the possible correct names, which is why its size is squared.

Measuring the uncertainty that remains

Alongside candidate counts, I calculated entropy, a measure of the uncertainty left in the answer.

If the probability of name x is p(x), define:

\[ H(X)=-\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x) \]

With N equally likely candidates, H(X) = log₂N. Eight candidates give 3 bits; 16 give 4 bits.

H(X | Z) is the uncertainty remaining after seeing the numbers. H(X | Z, I) is what remains after also learning the initial I.

Using equal weights for the 33,837 Japanese readings, and weights proportional to the listed counts for the 29,225 U.S. spellings, gives:

Information available U.S.: weighted by listed counts Japanese: readings weighted equally
None: H(X) 11.64 bits 15.05 bits
Numbers: H(X | Z) 4.96 bits 8.03 bits
Numbers and initial: H(X | Z, I) 1.72 bits 4.23 bits

Both columns use the rule that preserves 11, 22, and 33, with sex unspecified. Under the uniform Japanese evaluation, the numbers remove about 7.02 bits of uncertainty, and the initial removes a further 3.80 bits.

The two columns use different units and weights: readings versus spellings, and uniform versus frequency-based selection. They cannot establish that names in one language are inherently easier to guess. A frequency-weighted mean candidate count for the U.S. data would also use each name's listed count as its weight, rather than the uniform-average formula above.

Entropy alone doesn't determine how many guesses you need or the top-candidate success rate. Candidate counts, the distribution of probability, and how many guesses are allowed need to be examined separately.

Display and counting details

  • The maximum of 25: Section 6 uses the fixed Japanese dictionary, the original romanization only, the rule preserving 11, 22, and 33, and a specified initial. This is the size of the entire set selected for display before pagination, including cases where the tool falls back to the original dictionary because there are no priority candidates. Some conditions exceed 25 when the initial is unspecified, all numbers are reduced to one digit, or passport-style spellings are included.
  • Counts versus what is shown first: The combination browser shows the number of distinct matching names in the original dictionary. The Japanese name view shows priority candidates first, so fewer names may initially be visible than the combination count suggests. If there are no priority candidates, the original dictionary's candidates are shown instead.
  • Searching both spellings: Including passport-style spellings is off by default. Each reading counts as one candidate. Results display only the spellings that match the specified numbers and initial. If both conventions match, two spelling rows appear within that one candidate.
  • U.S. recorded sex and proportions: The default is unspecified, with male and female counts combined. Male and female refer to the SSA's recorded categories. When one is selected, the candidates are spellings listed in that category, and its counts determine the ranking. A spelling listed in both categories remains in both. For frequency weighting, the denominator is the sum of the selected-category counts across all matching candidates, not just the candidates on one screen page. Under equal weighting, every remaining candidate has the same weight. Appendices 1 and 2 report results with sex unspecified.
  • Names without vowels or without consonants: A missing component is shown as “—”, not replaced with 0 or 9. Including these cases gives 187 occupied combinations in the fixed Japanese dictionary under the rule preserving 11, 22, and 33.

Coverage of the name lists

Priority candidates are readings that also appear in a separate name list. The selection therefore depends on what that source includes and how its entries were chosen.

Names exist outside the lists. Neither the original dictionary nor the priority set covers every name. A name's absence from the screen does not establish that it doesn't exist.

Public-release update, 23 October 2026: During the research, appearances in rankings from Meiji Yasuda, Tamahiyo, and Baby Calendar also informed the selection. Because redistribution terms for that information in a public app had not been confirmed, it was removed from the public version's selection criteria. Priority candidates are now selected solely by matching against the MIT-licensed name lists below. The original dictionary of 33,837 readings and the calculation rules are unchanged.

What this experiment examines is how much information about a name survives the calculation that turns it into numbers. It does not test the validity of numerology's personality readings or predictions.
